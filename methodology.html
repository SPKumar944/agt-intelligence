01

Trade areas

A trade area is the geographic area from which a store draws most of its customers. Retail planning divides it into a primary zone (roughly 60–70% of customers), a secondary zone (the next 15–25%) and a tertiary fringe.

Historically these zones were drawn as distance rings or drive-time bands. We draw them from behaviour: hexagons are ranked by their share of a store's visitors and accumulated until each zone's share is reached, on real travel times and respecting barriers. Where visitor data is not available — a site that has not opened — a gravity model on population and travel time estimates the shape, validated against comparable stores.

02

Distance decay and the Huff model

The further a store is, the less likely people are to use it — but how fast that likelihood falls depends on the format. People walk a few minutes for coffee and drive half an hour for furniture. The distance-decay exponent β captures this: higher β means customers are more reluctant to travel.

The Huff model turns decay into competition. A customer between two stores splits their custom in proportion to each store's attractiveness divided by its distance raised to β. The break-even point is where the two are equally likely.

Move the sliders: with a steeper decay, each store dominates its own side more sharply; make store B more attractive and the break-even point moves towards store A.

03

Footfall modelling

Street-level footfall combines four effects: how much a road can carry, how strongly nearby places pull people, how much transit adds, and when in the day people move. Where aggregated mobility counts exist they calibrate the model; where they don't, the structure still ranks streets and hours reliably.

    Scoring

    A site score ranks locations against the whole market. Each driver is converted to a percentile — the share of locations in the study area that score lower — so drivers measured in different units (people per hour, rupees, number of rivals) become comparable.

    The score is a weighted average of those percentiles. Weights are set per business profile, written down with a rationale and shown to users, so they can be challenged and changed. Factors where more is worse, such as competitive pressure, are inverted before weighting.

    Percentiles are robust to outliers and need no sales history, which makes scoring the right tool for ranking candidates early. Its limitation is that it is relative: a score of 90 says a site is better than most, not how much it will sell. That is the job of the forecast.

    05

    Forecasting and uncertainty

    Revenue models learn the relationship between a store's surroundings and its sales from existing stores. Rather than a single number, they predict quantiles: P10, P50 and P90. A well-calibrated model sees outcomes fall between P10 and P90 about eight times in ten.

    Each prediction is explained with additive attributions — contributions that start from a format baseline and sum exactly to the forecast — so a committee can see which drivers add and which subtract.

    Ranges also reflect the ramp-up of a new store: early months carry more uncertainty than a mature run-rate.

    06

    Validation and ground truth

    A model is only as good as its last test. We validate at three levels before results reach a decision.

    Back-testing

    Forecasts are tested on stores held out of training, reporting percentage error and how often outcomes fall within the forecast range.

    On-ground counts

    Short pedestrian counts at shortlisted frontages, during the hours that matter to the format, calibrate modelled footfall where it counts most.

    Analyst review

    Trade areas, competitor matches and outliers are reviewed in QGIS by analysts who know the city before anything is released.

    Modelled versus measured. Every number in our reports is labelled as measured (an observed count or sale) or modelled (an estimate), with its source and date. Modelled numbers are for ranking and comparing; commitments should rest on numbers that have been checked on the ground.

    Glossary

    Catchment / trade areaThe area a store draws most of its customers from, divided into primary, secondary and tertiary zones.
    IsochroneThe area reachable within a given travel time by a given mode.
    Distance decayThe decline in likelihood of travelling to a place as travel time or distance increases.
    Huff modelA probabilistic model of store choice based on attractiveness and distance to every alternative.
    CannibalisationSales a new store takes from the same brand's existing stores.
    Sales transferThe amount, in currency, expected to move from an existing store to a new one.
    IncrementalityThe share of a new store's sales that are genuinely new to the network.
    WhitespaceAreas where target-customer demand is high and category supply is low.
    H3An open-source hierarchical hexagonal grid used to index all spatial data.
    PercentileThe share of locations in a market that score lower on a driver.
    P10 / P50 / P90Forecast quantiles: the outcome should exceed P10 nine times in ten and exceed P90 one time in ten.
    Back-testingTesting a model on data it was not trained on, such as held-out stores.
    Feature storeA versioned table of model inputs, shared by every model so they use identical data.
    FootfallThe number of people passing a location in a period; modelled or measured.
