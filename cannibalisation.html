The concept

Overlap is not the same as loss.

Two trade areas can overlap heavily and still both thrive, if the shared neighbourhood has more demand than one store can capture. And a modest overlap can be expensive if it covers the existing store's best customers.

That is why we measure sales transfer, not just geometric overlap. For every hexagon we estimate how its customers split their spending between your stores and the competition today, and how that split changes when the new store opens. The difference, summed over the map, is the revenue each existing store is expected to lose.

The figure that matters to the business is net new sales: the new store's forecast minus everything it takes from the network. A site with a smaller forecast and little cannibalisation can be worth more than a bigger site next door to an existing outlet.

The Huff model

How customers choose between stores.

The Huff model, introduced by David Huff in 1963, is the standard way to describe store choice. The probability that a customer in an area chooses a store rises with the store's attractiveness and falls with the effort of getting there — and depends on every alternative nearby, including competitors.

We calibrate attractiveness (size, format, brand) and distance decay per format, and use travel time on the real network rather than straight-line distance.

Method

How a study runs.

Map demand

Category spend per hexagon from households, workers and visitors, using the dynamic catchments of the stores involved.

Build the choice set

Your stores and every relevant competitor, with attractiveness from size, format and brand.

Calibrate

Distance decay and attractiveness are tuned until modelled sales match existing stores' actual sales.

Simulate the opening

Add the proposed store, recompute choice probabilities everywhere, and measure transfer from each store.

Compare candidates

Rank candidates by net incremental sales, and test spacing and size scenarios.

FAQ

Questions about cannibalisation.

Does it account for competitors?

Yes. Competitors are part of the choice set, so a new store can win share from them as well as from your own stores — which is exactly what makes some close-together openings worthwhile.

Can it handle many stores at once?

Yes. The model computes transfer from every existing store simultaneously, and can evaluate a whole expansion programme to find the best sequence and spacing.

What about online and delivery orders?

Delivery demand is modelled on its own catchment — the delivery radius — so overlap between delivery zones is reported separately from walk-in overlap.
