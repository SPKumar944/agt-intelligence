The concept

Why radius rings mislead.

A radius ring — "everyone within 3 km" — is the oldest shortcut in site selection. It assumes customers travel equally in every direction and that every household inside the circle is equally likely to visit. Neither is true.

Distance is not travel time. Two homes 2 km away can be five minutes apart along an arterial road or twenty-five minutes apart across a river with one bridge. People choose stores by the effort it takes to reach them, which is why trade areas stretch along fast roads and transit lines and stop short at rail lines, expressways and water.

Where people live is only half the story. A store near offices draws its weekday lunch trade from people who live elsewhere. Aggregated mobility data shows both footprints — night-time origins and day-time origins — and they are often very different shapes.

Rings hide empty ground. Lakes, parks, industrial land and gated campuses sit inside rings and send almost no one. Counting them inflates the population a site appears to serve.

A dynamic catchment replaces the circle with the smallest area that actually holds a given share of a store's customers, built on real travel times. It is the foundation for every other module: forecasts, cannibalisation and whitespace all start from it.

Method

How a trade area is built.

Five steps, the same for every site, so trade areas are comparable across a whole network.

Collect visitor origins

Aggregated mobility data gives the share of a location's visitors whose night-time and day-time locations fall in each hexagon. Where mobility data is thin, a gravity model on population and travel time fills the gap.

Compute travel times

A routable network of roads, footpaths and transit — with speeds by road class and time of day — gives the walking, driving and transit time from every hexagon to the site.

Respect barriers

Rivers, rail lines, expressways and closed campuses are crossed only where the network allows, so customers beyond a barrier are reached via the real bridge or underpass.

Draw capture contours

Hexagons are ranked by their share of customers and accumulated until the contour holds a target share — the primary trade area at about 60–70%, the secondary at 80–85%.

Validate

Shapes are checked by an analyst against local knowledge and, where available, loyalty or delivery addresses aggregated to hexagons.

Trade-area zones

Primary, secondary and tertiary.

FAQ

Questions about catchments.

Do you track individual devices?

No. Mobility data reaches us already aggregated to hexagons and time windows, with minimum-count thresholds. We never see or store individual movement traces.

What if a site is new and has no visitors yet?

We build the expected trade area from comparable stores of the same format and from a travel-time gravity model on population. Once the store opens, its observed footprint replaces the estimate.

Can we use our own customer addresses?

Yes. Loyalty or delivery addresses, aggregated to hexagons under your agreement, are the best possible validation and can replace modelled origins entirely.

Which capture share should we use?

We default to 70% for primary and 85% for secondary, a common convention. Destination formats often use wider bands; convenience formats narrower ones. The share is a setting, not a hidden assumption.
